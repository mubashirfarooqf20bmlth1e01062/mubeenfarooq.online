EDUCATION STAFFING · DISTRICT MARKET RESEARCH

How Education Staffing Companies Can Research School District Markets

Education staffing companies need more than a district list: they need evidence that the district fits their roles, service area, capacity and operating model. Research public hiring and procurement signals, map the people involved, and separate confirmed facts from assumptions before outreach.

By Research Ops Editorial Team · November 18, 2026 · 8–10 minute read

General business-development guidance only. Staffing, educator credentialing, background screening, labor, insurance and procurement requirements vary by state and district. Confirm current requirements with official sources and qualified professionals.

Define the staffing roles and service model first

“Education staffing” can cover very different services: substitute teachers, classroom aides and paraprofessionals, special education staff, related-service professionals, permanent recruitment, short-term coverage or a managed staffing arrangement. A district that needs a substitute pool is not necessarily a fit for a company focused on direct-hire recruiting or one specialized clinical role.

Before building a territory, write a concise capability profile. List the job families you actually fill, employment or placement model, states served, school settings, coverage limits, lead time, recruiting capacity, supervision or support approach, and exclusions. Use this profile as a filter—not a promise that every listed role is available for every district.

Build a district territory from official data

Start with geography and district type. The National Center for Education Statistics Common Core of Data (CCD) and its public school district locator provide an official starting point for identifying and checking public local education agencies. Use the Elementary/Secondary Information System (ElSi) to explore available district-level data and build comparisons.

Keep the account record simple: official district name, state, NCES identifier where available, official website, district type, location, service-area fit, source URL and date verified. Confirm district boundaries and operating status with current state or district sources; do not assume a city name, county boundary or school list is the same thing as the district's purchasing entity.

Segment accounts by the roles you can supply, geographic reach, school setting, and likely operational fit. A small, verified list of districts your recruiting team can realistically serve is more useful than a large national export that includes accounts outside your capacity.

Read hiring signals without assuming outsourced demand

Review official district employment pages, job postings, board agendas and minutes, budget documents, staffing plans, strategic plans, public contract registers and procurement notices. Search phrases should match your job families—such as substitute teacher, paraeducator, classroom aide, special education teacher, temporary staffing, staffing services or recruitment services—then record the exact source and date.

NCES's October 2024 School Pulse Panel release described hiring challenges heading into the 2024–25 school year and identified challenges across teaching and non-teaching roles. NCES labels this panel an experimental data product. Treat such national reporting as context for research—not a current forecast for an individual district.

A vacancy usually signals that a district is recruiting for a position; it does not establish that the district wants a staffing vendor. An agenda item may describe an internal hiring decision or an existing agreement. A past contract can help you understand the category and procurement route, but it is not proof of an active need. Label findings confirmed public fact, possible signal or unknown.

Map hiring, program and purchasing stakeholders

Do not assume one contact owns every staffing decision. Map roles first, then verify names and titles in official district directories.

  • Human resources or talent acquisition: recruitment processes, employment categories, onboarding and workforce planning.
  • Substitute or staffing coordinators: daily coverage models, candidate pools and scheduling workflows, where the district has such a role.
  • Program leadership: special education, student services, curriculum or other departments connected to the specific positions.
  • School administrators: local staffing needs and operational context; they may not control contracting.
  • Procurement, finance and contract administration: solicitation instructions, purchasing thresholds, approvals, vendor setup and contract terms.

Record the contact's public title, department, official source, date checked and role in the process. If you cannot confirm who owns a function, mark it unverified and ask the district through its published channel instead of guessing.

Verify readiness, credentials and operating fit

Before calling an account qualified, compare the district's published requirements with your actual ability to deliver. Depending on role and location, questions may include state educator or professional credentials, required background checks, fingerprinting, child-safety training, onboarding, supervision, insurance, work authorization, labor or contract terms, scheduling systems and reporting. This is a research checklist, not a legal or licensing interpretation.

Also check capacity: Can you recruit the requested job family in the district's geography? Can your team handle the likely schedule, school calendar, onboarding lead time and service locations? What happens if a placement does not start or a staff member is absent? Do not send a proposal until the staffing and operations team has confirmed the scope is realistic.

Research the district's procurement and vendor route

Find the official purchasing page and follow its current instructions. Look for open solicitations, vendor registration, bid calendars, cooperative purchasing, contract awards, renewal dates, question deadlines and addenda. Save the solicitation number, source link and deadline. Registration, interest forms and email alerts are not the same as approval or an award.

Archived examples can show what a district has bought before. Albuquerque Public Schools' archived “Temporary Staffing Services – As Needed” RFP and its substitute-teacher and educational-assistance staffing solicitation are examples of public records to study; their posted deadlines are historical, not active opportunities. Meanwhile, Fort Bend ISD's purchasing page explains its portal process and states that registration does not automatically approve a vendor. Check each district's current process rather than copying another district's steps.

For state context, the Texas Education Agency's purchasing FAQ notes that Texas districts make purchasing decisions locally or through cooperatives. That is a Texas-specific example—not a nationwide rule—and illustrates why a state-level vendor list may not replace district-level research.

Prioritize accounts and prepare relevant outreach

Use a transparent scorecard. For each account, assess service fit, territory and recruiting capacity, strength and freshness of public signals, verified stakeholder coverage, procurement timing, and operational readiness. Use simple high/medium/low ratings or a weighted score you can explain. Keep evidence and the reason for each rating visible; avoid false precision.

Hypothetical example: A staffing company that recruits substitute teachers and classroom aides in one state sees a district's official jobs page listing several openings and finds an older public staffing solicitation. It verifies the district, confirms its service area and capacity, maps HR and procurement roles, and checks whether any current solicitation exists. The account may merit a carefully researched inquiry, but the job listings and archived bid alone do not prove that a contract is available.

  • Confirm each target's district identity, service fit and recruiting reach.
  • Save official links and dates; distinguish live notices from historical records.
  • Map HR, program and procurement roles without treating any one contact as the buyer.
  • Check qualification, screening and onboarding questions before promising capacity.
  • Write outreach around a verified, relevant public fact and a concise question; respect the district's vendor-contact rules.
  • Track research completeness, outreach activity, responses and next steps separately from contracts or revenue.
