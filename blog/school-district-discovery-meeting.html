How to Prepare for a School District Discovery Meeting

A school district discovery meeting is a chance to understand the district’s context—not to force a pitch. Preparation helps a provider ask better questions, respect procurement boundaries and leave with a clear next step. This guide explains how to prepare using public evidence, a concise agenda and a realistic view of service fit.

By Research Ops Editorial Team · November 23, 2026 · 7–8 minute read

General business-development guidance only. District priorities, meeting protocols, procurement, privacy, service and implementation requirements vary by district, state, funding source and service category. Verify current requirements before making commitments.

Define the purpose of the school district discovery meeting

A useful discovery meeting is not a shortened sales presentation. It is a structured conversation to test whether a provider’s service may fit a district’s stated priorities, understand how the district evaluates outside support and agree on an appropriate next step. A meeting can be successful even when the answer is “not now” or “this is not the right route.”

Before accepting or requesting a meeting, write one sentence that explains why the conversation is relevant. For example: “We would like to understand how your student-services team evaluates outside providers for school-based occupational therapy and which public purchasing process applies.” Keep it specific, neutral and open to correction.

Decide what you need to learn—not what you want the district to promise. A practical objective might be to clarify the service owner, understand current delivery arrangements, identify operational constraints and learn whether the district has a formal route for considering providers. Do not treat an introductory conversation as an endorsement, procurement approval or contract opportunity.

Build a one-page briefing from verified public information

Prepare a concise account brief that the meeting team can scan in a few minutes. Confirm the district’s legal or commonly used name, state, official website and relevant department. The National Center for Education Statistics’ public school district locator is a useful starting point for checking district identity and its NCES identifier; cross-check details against the state education agency and the district’s own website.

Include only evidence that helps frame a respectful question:

  • District context: official mission, strategic priorities, service pages or public improvement plans that relate directly to your offer.
  • Relevant roles: department leadership, program contacts, procurement and other functions that may evaluate the service. Record the source and date each role was checked.
  • Public process: supplier registration instructions, active solicitations, board materials, purchasing guidance and published contact channels.
  • Your fit: service scope, geography, delivery model, capacity, relevant qualifications and the limits of what your organization can provide.

Separate confirmed facts from questions. A district plan that mentions student access, technology or inclusive instruction does not prove a particular service shortage, budget allocation or intent to outsource. Write “possible discussion area” rather than converting a broad public priority into an asserted need.

Invite the right participants and send a focused agenda

Ask who the district expects to attend and what each person’s role will be. Depending on the service, the conversation may involve a program director, special education or student-services leader, curriculum or technology staff, operations, finance or procurement. Do not assume one person can approve every operational and purchasing decision.

Send a short agenda in advance. For a 30-minute discussion, a simple structure could be:

  • 5 minutes — introductions, purpose and confirmation of the service area.
  • 10 minutes — district context, current approach and priorities described by the district.
  • 8 minutes — provider capability, delivery boundaries and questions about fit.
  • 5 minutes — procurement, review steps and information the district may need.
  • 2 minutes — recap, owners and a mutually agreed next action.

Adjust the timing to the district’s preference. Share a concise capability sheet only if useful, label assumptions, and avoid sending a lengthy unsolicited proposal. If the district has an active solicitation or a designated question process, use that process rather than trying to obtain additional information through an informal meeting.

Ask discovery questions that reveal fit and constraints

Open questions help the district describe its own situation. Choose a small number that match the service category rather than reading a generic questionnaire. Useful prompts include:

  • Which team coordinates this service area, and how are responsibilities divided?
  • How is the service currently delivered—internally, through an existing provider, through a shared service or another arrangement?
  • What outcomes or operational improvements matter most to the district?
  • Which students, schools, departments or locations are within the potential scope?
  • What capacity, scheduling, geographic, technology or implementation constraints should a provider understand?
  • Who reviews program fit, technical or privacy matters, funding and purchasing?
  • Is there a published procurement route, current contract or solicitation that providers should follow?
  • What information would be useful for the district to review next, and by when?

Listen for what is not yet known. The district may be gathering information, planning a future cycle, satisfied with an existing arrangement or unable to consider a new provider. Do not press for confidential details or interpret a polite response as an invitation to submit a proposal. Summarize what you heard and ask whether your understanding is accurate.

Respect procurement, privacy and professional boundaries

A discovery meeting does not bypass purchasing controls. Before the meeting, review the district’s official supplier and procurement information. Ask which process applies, whether vendor registration is only an administrative prerequisite, and whether questions must be submitted through a bid portal or named procurement contact. Requirements differ by district, state, service, contract value and funding source.

For transactions under a federal award or subaward, 2 CFR § 200.318 addresses documented procurement procedures, contractor oversight, conflicts of interest and procurement records. Its scope is federal-award procurement; it is not a single purchasing rule for every district transaction. Confirm which federal, state and local requirements apply to the specific opportunity.

Keep student information out of an introductory business-development discussion. Do not request names, education records, individualized education programs, evaluation reports, health information or other personally identifiable student details. The U.S. Department of Education explains that FERPA’s school-official exception for contractors is conditional: the outside party must perform an institutional function the school would otherwise use employees to perform, remain under the school’s direct control regarding education records, follow use and redisclosure limits and meet the district’s criteria. The Department recommends written agreements as a best practice before sharing personally identifiable information. Review its FERPA guidance for outside organizations and written-agreement guidance.

Clinical, special education compliance, licensing, insurance, accessibility and legal questions should be confirmed with the district and qualified professionals. Close with a clear next step and a written recap

Reserve the final minutes to confirm what happens next. State the district’s preferred route, the information it asked for, who owns each action and any date the parties agreed. If no action was agreed, record that accurately. A useful follow-up is a short factual recap—not an unsolicited proposal that assumes approval.

After the meeting, separate three kinds of notes:

  • Confirmed: facts the district stated or that are supported by a published source.
  • Open: questions requiring verification, internal review or a formal procurement response.
  • Next action: the named owner, due date and agreed channel for the next communication.

Update the account record with the meeting date, attendees and roles, source links, service line, procurement status, follow-up permission and next review date. Use precise stages such as “discovery held,” “procurement route to verify,” “information requested” or “proposal invited.” Do not label the district a client or partner unless a genuine award or agreement supports that description.

Hypothetical example: turn research into a useful meeting plan

Imagine an occupational therapy provider preparing for an introductory discussion with a public school district. The provider confirms the district identity, reads its public student-services page and finds a general strategic priority related to access and participation. It does not assume that the district has an OT vacancy or needs an outside contractor.

The provider sends a brief agenda asking to understand how OT services are coordinated, which team reviews outside support and where the district publishes supplier requirements. During the meeting, the district explains that program staff can discuss service context but procurement controls the purchasing route. The provider sends a concise recap and waits for the official process before submitting anything. The result is a verified next step—not a promised contract.
