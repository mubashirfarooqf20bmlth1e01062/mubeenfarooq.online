K-12 PIPELINE MANAGEMENT & PROPOSAL READINESS

From First Conversation to Proposal: Organizing a K-12 Opportunity Pipeline

A promising first conversation is not yet a qualified opportunity—and a submitted proposal is not a contract. A disciplined K-12 opportunity pipeline records what the district has actually confirmed, what remains unknown, who owns the next action and which procurement step controls the path forward.

By Research Ops Editorial Team · November 24, 2026 · 7–9 minute read

General business-development guidance only. District purchasing, proposal, contracting, privacy and implementation requirements vary by district, state, service category, contract value and funding source. Confirm the applicable process with the district before making commitments.

What a K-12 opportunity pipeline should make visible

A useful pipeline is a shared record of evidence, the district’s actual buying process and the next action both sides have agreed to take. It should answer four questions: Is this the correct district? Is there a plausible service fit? Has the district identified a legitimate next step? What must happen before a proposal can be considered?

Use observable stage names: “research verified,” “conversation held,” “discovery completed,” “procurement route confirmed,” “proposal invited” and “proposal submitted.” A friendly reply is not a discovery meeting. Vendor registration is not an award. A proposal under review is not approval. This guide focuses on stage gates and handoffs; for the underlying record system, see the separate school district CRM guide.

Stage 1: Research and qualify the district account

Verify the contracting entity before outreach. The NCES public school district locator and Common Core of Data help confirm public district identity and directory information. Cross-check state, name and official website against the state education agency and district pages; similar names or nearby counties do not prove two records are the same entity.

Build a short evidence brief for the service line: public program pages, relevant strategic priorities, official department contacts, purchasing instructions and genuinely related solicitations. Separate facts from interpretation. A plan mentioning student access does not prove a staffing gap, approved budget or intent to outsource.

  • Verify the contracting entity and service geography.
  • Confirm the provider’s real service model, coverage and capacity.
  • Record relevant public evidence, source links and review dates.
  • Identify an official contact route or procurement channel.
  • Flag disqualifiers and unknowns instead of hiding them.

Research Ops supports district account research and public-source decision-maker mapping. The goal is relevant outreach—not assumptions about private student needs.

Stage 2: Record the first conversation accurately

When a district contact responds or agrees to speak, record the date, participants and verified roles; the topic discussed; the source of the introduction; the district’s questions; and the next action actually accepted. If the contact refers you to procurement or says they are not the right person, update the route.

Distinguish an attempted contact, a reply, a brief introductory exchange and substantive discovery. “Send information” does not necessarily mean the district has a defined project. Ask what would be useful, where it should go and whether a published process applies. Give each open record one owner, one next action and a due date. If the next step depends on the district, mark it as waiting on district response.

Stage 3: Complete discovery and qualify the buying path

Discovery should test operational fit and purchasing reality before proposal work begins. Ask the district to explain the context rather than presenting assumptions as facts:

  • Which department coordinates the service, and who evaluates program fit?
  • How is the service delivered today, and what would an outside provider need to complement?
  • What scope, locations, schedule or implementation conditions are being considered?
  • Who reviews qualifications, technology, privacy, insurance and other requirements?
  • What purchasing route applies, and is a proposal appropriate now?

Mark answers as confirmed, tentative or unanswered. A program contact may describe a challenge but have no authority to select a vendor; procurement may control the competitive process. The Seattle Public Schools procurement page illustrates how a district may publish current solicitations, vendor expectations and purchasing information in one official place. It is a local example, not a nationwide rule.

Exit criteria: service fit is understood, relevant stakeholders are mapped, the purchasing route is verified or clearly marked unresolved, and the district has identified an appropriate next step. Otherwise, keep the account in discovery or nurture.

Stage 4: Move to proposal only when the district is ready

Use “proposal invited” or “proposal requested” as a separate stage before “proposal submitted.” That prevents an unsolicited capability deck from being mistaken for a formal bid. For an active solicitation, follow its scope, format, deadlines, question process and submission channel.

For an invited proposal, state the service and locations, delivery model, schedule assumptions, responsibilities, implementation dependencies, reporting, qualifications, pricing basis, term, exclusions and items requiring district confirmation. Match the response to the stated evaluation criteria. Do not promise capacity, outcomes, integrations or start dates that the responsible provider team has not validated.

  • Confirm the response answers the district’s actual request.
  • Approve scope, price, capacity, qualifications and attachments internally.
  • Make assumptions, dependencies and exclusions explicit.
  • Check the deadline, submission channel and receipt confirmation.
  • Assign privacy, accessibility, insurance and technical reviews to qualified staff.

Store the submitted version, timestamp, delivery method and confirmation. Keep drafts separate so the team knows exactly what the district received.

Stage 5: Track procurement, decision and handoff separately

After submission, use factual statuses such as “acknowledged,” “under evaluation,” “clarification requested,” “not selected,” “award notice received” or “agreement executed.” Record only what the district or official portal confirms. An award notice, signed agreement and authorization to begin work may be distinct milestones. Do not schedule delivery or describe the organization as a partner until required authorization is in place.

District rules are not interchangeable. Seattle Public Schools publishes a vendor code emphasizing fair competition and ethical conduct; review each district’s own procurement guidance rather than assuming another district’s process applies.

If the service or technology will involve student education records, pause for district privacy and security review. The U.S. Department of Education’s FERPA resources explain the federal privacy framework. As a local example, NYC Public Schools’ outside-entity guidance describes written agreements and data-protection conditions for certain third parties; it is not a universal vendor approval list. Do not place student-identifiable information in a prospecting spreadsheet or general business-development CRM.

When an opportunity closes, record the outcome and any reason the district shared. “No current fit,” “timing deferred” and “not selected” are more useful than a vague “lost.” Set a future review only when there is a legitimate reason and a respectful follow-up basis.

Hypothetical example: an OT provider moves toward a proposal

Imagine an occupational therapy provider that verifies a district and reviews its public student-services page. A program contact agrees to an introductory call. The provider records “conversation held,” not “qualified buyer.” During discovery, the district explains that program staff discuss scope but any new purchase must follow its published procurement route.

The provider checks its coverage and capacity, records the procurement contact and waits. Later, the district issues a relevant solicitation. The provider reviews the instructions, completes an internal bid/no-bid check and submits through the stated channel. The pipeline now shows “proposal submitted”—not “contract won.” Only a confirmed award and required agreement or authorization support the next status.
