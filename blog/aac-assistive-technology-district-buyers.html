AAC and Assistive Technology: Mapping K-12 Decision-Makers

AAC and assistive technology decisions in K–12 are rarely made by one buyer. A communication app, speech-generating device, access tool or training package may involve educators, SLPs, assistive technology specialists, IT, families and procurement. Vendors that map those roles before outreach can ask better questions—without treating public signals as proof of student need or an open purchase.

By Research Ops Editorial Team · November 15, 2026 · 7–9 minute read

Define the AAC and assistive technology offer first

“AAC” and “assistive technology” are related, but they are not one product category. Augmentative and alternative communication can include gestures, signs, picture or letter boards, communication books, apps and speech-generating devices. ASHA describes AAC as a flexible system that may combine unaided, low-tech and high-tech methods. The right communication approach depends on the individual and the environments where communication happens.

Before building a district list, state exactly what your organization provides: devices, software, accessories, access tools, implementation support, staff training, maintenance or technical assistance. Clarify what is included, what requires a qualified clinician or assistive-technology professional, and what you do not provide. Do not position a product demonstration as an individualized assessment or a substitute for professional judgment. See ASHA’s AAC Practice Portal for school-team and implementation context.

Build a reliable K-12 district research universe

Define where you can genuinely support a district: states served, implementation coverage, language support, product compatibility, training capacity and technical support. A national prospect list is not useful if your organization cannot support the locations on it.

The NCES 2025–26 Common Core of Data preliminary directory provides basic public-school and local education agency information, including NCES identifiers, location, agency type, operational status and grade span. The release is based on a snapshot date of October 1, 2025. Use it to structure the account universe, then verify current district status, boundaries and official websites with state and district sources. Record the source and date checked.

Map the AAC decision-making group by function

Do not assume one person is “the AAC buyer.” Map the people who understand program needs, support implementation, review technology and manage purchasing. Titles differ by district, and one person may hold more than one role.

  • Program leadership: special education, exceptional student services, student support or related-services directors may understand program priorities and service models.
  • Communication expertise: a lead SLP, AAC specialist or speech-language coordinator may understand assessment practices, communication goals and professional implementation.
  • Assistive technology: an AT coordinator or specialist may review devices, software, access methods, trials and training requirements.
  • Daily implementation: educators, paraprofessionals, SLPs, occupational and physical therapists and family partners can describe classroom use, positioning and communication across settings.
  • Technology and privacy: IT, information security, instructional technology and accessibility staff may review compatibility, account management, data handling and support.
  • Purchasing and approval: procurement, business services, finance and contracts staff explain vendor registration, solicitation rules and approval steps. Superintendent or board review may apply under local policy.

ASHA describes school-based AAC as a team activity involving SLPs, educators, occupational and physical therapists, administrators, nurses, case managers and families. A clinical champion, implementation partner and authorized purchasing contact are not necessarily the same person.

Use public signals to frame questions—not infer need

Review official district pages for special education, speech-language services, assistive technology, accessibility, instructional technology and professional learning. Also check strategic plans, board agendas, public budgets, procurement portals, bid notices, awarded contracts and staff directories. Useful search terms may include AAC, communication supports, speech-generating devices, assistive technology, accessible materials, device trials and staff training.

Record what the source actually says, why it may be relevant and what remains unknown. A staff-training initiative does not prove a plan to buy software. A technology plan may concern network infrastructure rather than AAC. An existing contract may show an incumbent supplier, not an open opportunity. Never use student names, IEPs or confidential communication profiles for prospecting.

Understand IDEA’s assistive technology context

Under 34 CFR §300.324, an IEP Team must consider whether a child needs assistive technology devices and services. Under 34 CFR §300.105, a public agency must make them available when required as part of the child’s special education, related services or supplementary aids and services.

An assistive technology service is broader than handing over equipment. 34 CFR §300.6 includes support related to evaluation, acquisition, selection, customization, coordination, maintenance and training for a child, family or involved professionals. The Department of Education’s assistive technology guidance also discusses implementation and state AT programs, including device demonstrations and loans.

These provisions do not mean every district must buy a particular vendor’s product. IEP decisions are individualized, while purchasing follows separate district processes. Offer accurate product information and implementation support; do not promise an IEP outcome or try to influence an individual student’s decision.

Prepare for technology, privacy and procurement review

For connected devices or cloud-based apps, prepare clear answers about supported systems, accessibility, account setup, updates, offline use, technical support, data collection, retention, deletion and security controls. Ask the district which IT, accessibility and privacy reviews apply. Do not request student information during a sales discussion or demonstration unless the district has authorized a properly governed process.

The Department of Education’s FERPA guidance for online tools explains conditions that apply when providers receive personally identifiable information under the school-official exception, including school control over use and maintenance and limits on unauthorized use or redisclosure. Ask the district to define its required agreement and obtain qualified legal and security advice about your own obligations.

Check the district’s vendor portal, bid calendar, solicitation documents, contract templates and approval rules. Ask procurement which route applies before sending pricing or a proposal. Requirements vary by district, state, funding source and purchase.

Prioritize district accounts with an evidence-based map

Track territory fit, relevant public information, contact quality, implementation readiness, technology requirements, procurement visibility and the date each item was checked. Mark information as verified, needs confirmation or unknown. A gap in public information is a research task—not proof that a district has no need.

Hypothetical example: An AAC software provider identifies two districts in a state it can serve. District A publicly lists an assistive technology team and a vendor registration portal. District B has a general technology plan but no published AAC contact or purchasing information. District A may be the better first research priority because both a relevant program function and a purchasing route are visible. Neither signal proves a need, budget or intent to buy.

  • Confirm state and district serviceability.
  • Separate product, software, training and support capabilities.
  • Map program, implementation, IT/privacy and procurement functions.
  • Use public sources; keep student information out of prospect records.
  • Verify district-specific accessibility, technology and vendor requirements.
  • Respect published contact and solicitation rules.
