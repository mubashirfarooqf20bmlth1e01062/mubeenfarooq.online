School-Based OT Market Expansion: A District Research Checklist

School-based OT market expansion is not simply adding district names to a spreadsheet. Providers need to understand where they can operate, how their services fit school settings, which public signals merit follow-up and how each district evaluates outside providers. A disciplined research process helps teams prioritize realistic opportunities without assuming a district has an unmet need.

By Research Ops Editorial Team · November 9, 2026 · 7–9 minute read

Why school OT market expansion starts with educational context

School-based occupational therapy should be researched in the context of student participation and educational access—not marketed as a generic clinic service transferred into a school building. The American Occupational Therapy Association (AOTA) describes school-based practitioners as supporting children’s development and learning at school. AOTA also highlights collaborative, student-centered goals that can support participation in an educational program.

Federal IDEA regulations include occupational therapy among related services and define it in relation to qualified occupational therapists and specified functional purposes. This is useful background, but it does not tell a vendor that a particular district needs an outside provider, how many hours it may purchase or which purchasing route it will use. Individual student decisions and service plans belong to qualified professionals and the school’s applicable processes.

For business development, ask whether your organization can credibly serve the district’s geography, service model and stated purchasing process. Keep verified facts, reasonable hypotheses and unanswered questions separate.

Define territory and delivery readiness before prospecting

Start with your actual operating footprint. List the states and regions where you can deliver services today. Occupational therapy practice is regulated at the state level, so verify current licensure and authorization requirements with each relevant state licensing authority and qualified compliance advisers. Do not assume a credential automatically transfers across state lines.

Document the service model you can genuinely support: on-site services, telehealth where permitted and appropriate, evaluation, consultation, direct intervention or a combination. Add travel radius, school-day availability, language capacity, supervision arrangements, documentation workflows and realistic team capacity. If a new territory requires recruiting, credentialing or technology changes, label it as future readiness—not a current capability.

Build a reliable district universe from public data

The National Center for Education Statistics (NCES) Common Core of Data (CCD) is a useful starting point for identifying public school districts and standardizing basic directory information. NCES describes CCD as its primary database on public elementary and secondary education, including schools and school districts. Its search tools and downloadable files can help organize district names, locations and identifiers.

Do not treat a national dataset as a live contact directory. Data correspond to particular school years and may not reflect a recent reorganization, leadership change or website update. After selecting an account, verify its current name, status, official website, central office and relevant department pages on the district’s own site. Record the NCES identifier where useful, plus the source URL and verification date.

Decide which public entities belong in your strategy. Depending on the state and service model, you may research local education agencies, public charter LEAs, regional education service agencies or other public entities. Confirm each entity’s role and purchasing authority rather than assuming every network buys services in the same way.

Identify OT-relevant public signals without guessing at demand

Review public information that can support a thoughtful question: special education and student services pages, related-services descriptions, strategic plans, board agendas, approved budgets, public staffing notices, vendor pages and open solicitations. These sources may help explain how a district describes its programs, who coordinates services or where it publishes purchasing information.

Interpret each signal conservatively. A job posting may show recruitment activity, but it does not prove the district wants a contractor. A strategic plan may describe an educational priority without naming OT as a solution. A board agenda may record a past decision rather than an open opportunity. Label findings as a direct procurement signal, contextual clue or unverified hypothesis. Save the source, publication date and review date so another team member can reproduce the research.

Use public professional information only. Do not collect student names, individualized education programs, evaluations or other confidential records to build a commercial lead list. If a discussion moves toward student information, pause and have privacy, contracting and legal requirements reviewed by qualified professionals.

Map program contacts and the purchasing path separately

District titles and reporting lines vary. A program conversation might involve a special education director, related-services administrator, occupational therapy lead, student services leader or building administrator. Purchasing may sit with a procurement department, business office, central administration or formal solicitation portal. Treat these as roles to verify, not a universal org chart.

Create two tracks in your account brief:

  • Program track: who can explain the district’s service model, operational context and appropriate process for discussing OT services?
  • Purchasing track: who can clarify vendor registration, solicitation rules, insurance requirements, contract review and authorized purchasing steps?

Verify names and titles on official district pages before outreach. If the correct contact is unclear, ask the district’s published main office or procurement contact to direct you. The school district decision-maker guide explains why program influence and purchasing authority should not be treated as interchangeable.

Prioritize accounts with a transparent research scorecard

Rank accounts by fit and evidence, not by district size or the number of names in a database. Use a high, medium or low rating for each factor and write one sentence explaining it:

  • Geographic fit: can you serve the district with your current or clearly defined expansion footprint?
  • Service fit: does your actual OT model align with the public-facing programs and settings you researched?
  • Evidence quality: are the signals recent, direct and traceable to an official source?
  • Contact clarity: have you identified a current professional role and a legitimate route for an initial inquiry?
  • Procurement clarity: can you find vendor instructions, a purchasing contact or a formal solicitation route?
  • Operational readiness: can your organization address likely credentialing, staffing and onboarding questions if invited to continue?

Hypothetical example and a practical district checklist

Imagine an OT provider serving one state that is considering a neighboring market. Its research finds a district student-services page and a separate procurement page explaining how vendors receive bid notices. It finds no current OT solicitation and no public statement of unmet need. The provider first verifies its ability to operate in the state, confirms the district’s program contact and reads the vendor instructions. It then sends a concise inquiry asking which department can explain the appropriate process for prospective school-based OT providers. This is a hypothetical workflow, not a claim about a real district or an expected result.

Before moving an account into outreach, check that your brief includes:

  • Current district name, type, state and official website.
  • NCES identifier or another reliable public identifier, where applicable.
  • Verified service footprint and state authorization review.
  • OT service model, capacity and known delivery limitations.
  • Public signals with source links, dates and evidence labels.
  • Program contact and procurement route, with verification dates.
  • Open questions, next action and owner.

Sources and next steps for school-based OT market expansion

Use primary references when building your plan. These resources provide background, not a substitute for current state licensing guidance, district procurement instructions, clinical judgment or legal advice:

  • AOTA: School-based settings — school systems and resources for school-based OT practice.
  • U.S. Department of Education: IDEA regulation, 34 CFR § 300.34 — the related-services definition, including occupational therapy.
  • NCES: Common Core of Data — public school and district data, tools and documentation.
  • NCES: CCD data tools — district and school search tools and related data resources.
